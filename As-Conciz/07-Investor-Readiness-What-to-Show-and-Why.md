## 0. Read This First — The Honest Position You're In

Before the checklist, three things a good advisor would tell you plainly:

1. **You currently have zero product, zero users, zero revenue on As Conciz.** Everything so far is documentation. That is a completely normal place to start, but it means the honest fundraising stage here is **pre-seed / idea stage**, not seed. Do not present As Conciz as if it's further along than it is — experienced investors detect this instantly and it costs you the meeting, not just the round.

2. **The single highest-leverage action is not a better deck — it's a working demo.** For a product whose entire pitch is "this is faster than opening a chat window," a 20-second screen recording proves more than 20 slides. Everything else in this document is secondary to that.

3. **You are already fundraising for Delta** (₹2.5 Cr target, Hub71, multiple VC applications, ISB Ventures). **Any serious investor will ask why you're starting a second product while raising for the first.** This is the hardest question you will face and you need a real answer, not a deflection — options are: (a) As Conciz is an Alpha.Kore studio product with a separate owner, and you remain CEO on Delta; (b) you're deliberately transitioning focus and Delta has a defined path (partnership/sale, which you've already been evaluating); or (c) you don't raise for both simultaneously. Pick one and be able to say it in one sentence. "We're doing both" without structure reads as unfocused, and focus is one of the top things early-stage investors screen for.

## 2. What Professional Investors Will Specifically Expect From You

### 2.1 Numbers you must know cold, without checking notes
- Active users, DAU/WAU, retention at day 7 and day 30
- Cost per query and cost per user per month (you have this: $0.06–$0.45)
- Gross margin at your intended price point
- Burn rate and runway in months
- Cap table percentages
- If you have any revenue: MRR, churn, ARPU

Not knowing these live in a meeting is one of the most common and most damaging failure modes at this stage. "I'll get back to you" on your own burn rate signals you're not running the company closely.

### 2.2 Questions they will ask — prepare one-sentence answers
1. **"What happens when Microsoft or Apple builds this into the OS?"** — This is the hardest question for As Conciz specifically and it *will* be asked. Your honest answer: OS vendors move slowly on cross-application context, and the incumbent products already in market (Copilot at 8% voluntary preference) show that distribution doesn't guarantee usage. But don't pretend the risk isn't real.
2. **"Why won't people just use Copilot/Comet — they're free?"** — Your answer is friction (no context switch) plus reliability (deterministic math, untrusted-content handling). Prove it in the demo.
3. **"Why are you doing this while raising for Delta?"** — See §0.3. Have this answer ready before anything else.
4. **"What's your wedge? Who's your first 100 users?"** — Not "knowledge workers." A specific, reachable group: e.g. developers, or data analysts, or research students at specific institutions.
5. **"What if you don't raise?"** — Correct answer is that you build it anyway. Conditional commitment reads badly.
6. **"Who owns the IP?"** — If built under Alpha.Kore, clarify the relationship between the studio and this product. Ambiguity here is a real deal-blocker.
7. **"Why India-based, and what's your market?"** — India is 19% of global AI-app users but mobile-first; your product is desktop-only. Know this tension and have your segment answer ready (desktop-heavy professionals, not mass consumer).

### 2.3 What they expect operationally from a "professional body"
- Responsive, structured communication (data room link, not scattered attachments)
- A single shared data room (Google Drive/Notion) with: deck, demo video, financial model, cap table, incorporation docs, founder bios
- Clear, non-inflated claims — your stored preference for honest positioning over inflated claims is genuinely an asset here; investors reward founders whose numbers hold up under questioning
- Follow-up discipline — monthly investor updates even before they've invested, showing progress. This alone separates you from most applicants.

## 3. Why Would They Fund This — The Actual Case

Present these in this order. These are the real, evidence-backed reasons, not pitch language:

1. **The incumbents are large but demonstrably weak on trust.** Copilot: accuracy NPS at -19.8, only 8% voluntary preference when employees can choose, and Microsoft's own terms warned against relying on it. Comet: four separate disclosed security incidents in 12 months, all from the same root cause. Big distribution ≠ a defended position.
2. **The category has proven demand and a proven failure mode — and you've correctly identified both.** People clearly want "AI that understands what I'm looking at" (Copilot's 420M MAU, Comet's growth prove demand). What's failed is hardware-first delivery (Humane dead, Rabbit strained). You're taking the demand without the failure mode.
3. **The unit economics genuinely work.** At small-model tier, inference is $0.06–$0.45 per user per month against a subscription price. Very few pre-seed AI products can show a defensible gross margin this early. Most can't answer the cost question at all.
4. **The technical risk is integration risk, not research risk.** Every mechanism is documented, existing tech (UI Automation, macOS Accessibility API, global hotkeys, Stripe). Nothing here requires a breakthrough. Investors fund execution risk far more readily than research risk.
5. **The team has shipped before, bootstrapped.** 11 people, zero outside funding, real client delivery. That's the single most credible founder signal available to you and it's true.
6. **The pivot itself is evidence of judgment.** You started with a hardware concept, researched the precedents, and killed the hardware before spending a rupee on it. Frame this deliberately — it shows you kill your own bad ideas fast, which is exactly the trait investors are screening for.

## 4. What Would Make Them Say No — Fix These Before Pitching

- **No demo.** Fatal for this product specifically.
- **No users.** Survivable at pre-seed, but it caps you to "interesting, come back when you have usage."
- **Divided focus between Delta and As Conciz with no clear structure.** Most likely reason a well-run diligence process passes on you.
- **Overstated traction.** One inflated number that doesn't survive a follow-up question destroys credibility on everything else you said.
- **No answer to the OS-incumbent question.** "We'll be faster than Microsoft" is not an answer.
- **Unclear IP/entity structure between Alpha.Kore and As Conciz.**
- **A $8/month global price with no India-specific pricing thinking**, given ~50% of Indian adopters cite price as the primary factor and every major competitor is free there.

## 5. Recommended Sequence (next 8–10 weeks)

1. **Weeks 1–4:** Build the MVP — hotkey trigger + text/browser summarization on one OS. Nothing else. No CSV, no folders, no second platform.
2. **Week 4:** Record the demo video. This is the asset everything else hangs on.
3. **Weeks 4–6:** Get 20–50 real users. Friends, your own team, your vlog audience, college network. Instrument usage from day one.
4. **Week 6:** Build the deck around the demo and the real usage numbers — not before.
5. **Weeks 6–8:** Resolve entity/IP/cap table questions and assemble the data room.
6. **Weeks 8–10:** Start investor conversations, and in parallel prepare the YC Winter 2027 application (estimated deadline late Oct/early Nov 2026 — confirm on ycombinator.com/apply).

Do not invert this order. A deck built before a demo, before users, is a document about a hypothesis. A deck built after is a document about a product.
